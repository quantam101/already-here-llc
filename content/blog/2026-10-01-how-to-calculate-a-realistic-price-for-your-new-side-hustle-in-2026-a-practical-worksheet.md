## You’re Stuck on What to Charge – Here’s What You’ll Achieve  
You’ve identified a service you can deliver or a digital product you can sell, but when a client asks “How much?”, you feel forced to guess. By the end of this guide you will:  

1. Have a ready‑to‑use pricing worksheet that translates your costs, risk, and market position into a concrete price.  
2. Know how to test that price against early customers and adjust it on a regular schedule.  

No more wild guesses, no more under‑charging, and no more pricing paralysis.  

## 1. Map the Core Drivers of Your Price  
Every side‑hustle price can be broken down into six measurable inputs. Treat each as a column in a simple spreadsheet; the rows will be the individual projects or product units you sell.  

| Driver | What It Means | How to Measure |
|--------|---------------|----------------|
| **Delivery Time** | Hours (or days) you spend from order to fulfillment. | Track time on a few pilot jobs or use a time‑tracking app. |
| **Direct Costs** | Money you spend that is directly tied to the order (software licenses, raw materials, subcontractor fees). | Keep receipts or pull invoices for each cost type. |
| **Risk Premium** | Compensation for uncertainty (client revisions, missed deadlines, non‑payment). | Assign a percentage based on how volatile the work is. |
| **Demand Factor** | How eager the market is for your offering. | Use informal surveys, competitor pricing, or pre‑sale sign‑ups. |
| **Minimum Acceptable Margin** | The profit percentage you need to sustain the side‑hustle. | Decide a target (e.g., 30 %). |
| **Market Positioning** | The price tier you want to occupy (budget, mid‑range, premium). | Choose a multiplier based on the perceived value you deliver. |

Understanding each driver prevents you from relying on intuition alone.  

## 2. Gather Real Data Before You Plug Numbers In  

1. **Log a Test Batch** – Offer the service to 2‑3 friends or early adopters at a “beta” price. Record every minute spent and every expense incurred.  
2. **Collect Market Signals** – Browse the top 5 competitors on platforms like Fiverr, Upwork, or niche forums. Note their headline prices and any tiered packages.  
3. **Survey Potential Buyers** – A short Google Form asking “What would you pay for X?” can reveal willingness to pay. Even a 10‑response sample gives a direction.  

If any data point feels fuzzy, note it as an assumption to revisit later.  

## 3. Build a Simple Pricing Formula  

The formula below combines the six drivers into a single price per unit (service engagement or digital product).  

```
Base Price = (Delivery Time × Hourly Rate) + Direct Costs
Adjusted Price = Base Price × (1 + Risk Premium + Demand Factor + Positioning Multiplier)
Final Price = Adjusted Price / (1 – Minimum Acceptable Margin)
```

**Explanation of each term**

* **Hourly Rate** – Your personal “time value.” For a side‑hustle you might start at $25‑$40/hr depending on skill level.  
* **Risk Premium** – Add 5‑15 % if you anticipate revisions, late payments, or high client turnover.  
* **Demand Factor** – Add 0 % for a saturated market, up to 20 % if demand is strong and supply is thin.  
* **Positioning Multiplier** – 0 % for budget, 10‑20 % for mid‑range, 30 %+ for premium branding.  
* **Minimum Acceptable Margin** – If you need a 30 % net margin, use 0.30 in the denominator.  

The denominator ensures the margin is calculated after all adjustments, not just on top of costs.  

## 4. Worked Example – “Social‑Media Post Design” Side‑Hustle  

**Assumptions** (clearly labeled as an example)

| Item | Value |
|------|-------|
| Delivery Time | 3 hours per post |
| Hourly Rate | $30/hr |
| Direct Costs | $5 (stock image license) |
| Risk Premium | 10 % (client may request 2 revisions) |
| Demand Factor | 15 % (few designers in niche) |
| Positioning Multiplier | 10 % (mid‑range branding) |
| Minimum Acceptable Margin | 30 % |

**Step‑by‑step calculation**

1. **Base Price** = (3 hrs × $30) + $5 = $95  
2. **Sum of Percent Adjustments** = 10 % + 15 % + 10 % = 35 % → 0.35  
3. **Adjusted Price** = $95 × (1 + 0.35) = $95 × 1.35 = $128.25  
4. **Final Price** = $128.25 / (1 – 0.30) = $128.25 / 0.70 ≈ **$183**  

Rounded to a clean figure, you could list the service at **$180 per post**.  

**What the numbers tell you**

* Your time and direct costs alone would suggest $95, but the market realities (risk, demand, positioning) push the price up by nearly double.  
* The 30 % margin ensures you cover taxes, platform fees, and still have profit to reinvest.  

If a client balks at $180, you now have a transparent breakdown to discuss, or you can test a lower‑tier “basic” package that removes revisions (reducing risk premium).  

## 5. Create Your Own Pricing Worksheet  

Open a new Google Sheet (or Excel) and copy the layout below. Fill in the rows for each service or product you plan to sell.  

```
A: Service / Product Name
B: Delivery Time (hrs)
C: Hourly Rate ($)
D: Direct Costs ($)
E: Risk Premium (%)
F: Demand Factor (%)
G: Positioning Multiplier (%)
H: Minimum Acceptable Margin (%)
I: Base Price (=B*C + D)
J: Adjusted Price (=I * (1 + E/100 + F/100 + G/100))
K: Final Price (=J / (1 - H/100))
```

**Tips for using the sheet**

* **Lock formulas** in columns I‑K so you only edit the input columns (B‑H).  
* **Color‑code** rows where the final price exceeds a threshold you consider “premium” – it helps you see where positioning may be too high.  
* **Add a “Notes” column** to capture assumptions you need to verify (e.g., “Risk premium based on 2 revisions”).  

## 6. Set a Review Cadence – Keep Pricing Fresh  

Even a well‑calculated price will drift as costs, competition, and demand change. Adopt a simple cadence:  

| Review Frequency | What to Check | How to Act |
|------------------|---------------|-----------|
| **Weekly (first month)** | Time tracking accuracy, any unexpected costs. | Adjust Hourly Rate or Direct Costs in the sheet. |
| **Monthly (months 2‑6)** | New competitor pricing, client feedback on price fairness. | Tweak Risk Premium, Demand Factor, or Positioning Multiplier. |
| **Quarterly (after 6 months)** | Overall profitability, margin hit, market shifts. | Re‑evaluate Minimum Acceptable Margin and possibly raise or lower all prices. |

Document each review in a separate “Pricing Log” tab: date, changes made, reason, and impact on revenue. This log becomes evidence for future pricing decisions and protects you from arbitrary changes.  

## 7. Tradeoffs, Risks, and Failure Modes  

| Issue | Why It Happens | Mitigation |
|-------|----------------|------------|
| **Under‑estimating Time** | New operators often forget hidden steps (client onboarding, revisions). | Track every task for the first 5 projects; add a buffer (e.g., 10 %). |
| **Over‑pricing Early** | Premium positioning can scare away the first customers needed for validation. | Launch a limited “beta” price with a clear expiry date, then move to the calculated price. |
| **Ignoring Market Feedback** | Sticking to the worksheet despite repeated push‑back can stall growth. | Use the review cadence to adjust Demand Factor quickly. |
| **Margin Erosion from Platform Fees** | Selling on marketplaces may incur 10‑20 % fees that weren’t in Direct Costs. | Add platform fees to Direct Costs column before calculating. |
| **Scope Creep** | Clients ask for extra deliverables not captured in the original estimate. | Define a clear scope in the contract; any extra work triggers a new pricing worksheet. |

Understanding these pitfalls lets you act proactively rather than reacting after a loss.
