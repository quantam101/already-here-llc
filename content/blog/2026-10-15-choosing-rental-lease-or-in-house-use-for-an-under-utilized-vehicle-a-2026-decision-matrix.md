## Your vehicle sits idle several days a week – what should you do with it?  

If you own a delivery van, service truck, or even a fleet of e‑scooters that spend a third of each week parked, you’re likely wondering whether to keep it in‑house, rent it out to another business, or lease it to a third‑party operator. By the end of this guide you will be able to:

* Gather the exact cost and revenue numbers you need for each option.  
* Run a simple break‑even calculation that tells you the minimum utilization required to make renting or leasing worthwhile.  
* Use a ready‑made decision matrix to pick the option that aligns with your risk tolerance, administrative capacity, and growth goals.  

The process below works for any vehicle type—van, box truck, or delivery scooter—and can be completed in a single afternoon.

## Step 2: Build the Break‑Even Model for Each Option  

### 2.1 In‑House Use  

When you keep the vehicle in‑house you incur the full cost of ownership but also retain the flexibility to deploy it whenever needed. The break‑even point is simply the **minimum weekly mileage** that justifies the fixed costs.

**Formula**  
```
Weekly Cost = (Depreciation + Insurance + Maintenance + Fuel per mile × Weekly Miles) / 52
```
If the vehicle generates revenue (e.g., delivery fees) that exceeds this weekly cost, it is profitable to keep in‑house.

### 2.2 Renting to a Third Party  

Renting transfers most operating costs to the renter, but you must cover insurance, a portion of maintenance, and administrative overhead.

**Formula**  
```
Weekly Rental Profit = (Rental Rate × Rental Days) – (Insurance_additional + Maintenance_share + Admin_cost)
```
*Maintenance_share* can be a flat $50 / week or a percentage of mileage, depending on the agreement.

The break‑even rental days per week are:
```
Break‑Even Days = (Insurance_additional + Maintenance_share + Admin_cost) / Rental Rate
```

### 2.3 Leasing to a Fleet Operator  

Leasing provides a predictable cash flow but locks the vehicle into a fixed term, often with mileage caps.

**Formula**  
```
Weekly Lease Profit = (Lease Rate / 4) – (Insurance_additional + Maintenance_share)
```
(Assuming a 4‑week month.)

The break‑even weekly mileage for a lease is:
```
Required Mileage = (Lease Rate / 4 – Insurance_additional – Maintenance_share) / Fuel per mile
```
If the lease rate exceeds your total cost of ownership, the lease is automatically profitable regardless of mileage.

*How to use*: Multiply each score by its weight, sum the column, and compare totals. The highest total indicates the best overall fit, but you can also prioritize a single criterion (e.g., cash‑flow) by giving it a higher weight.

## Step 4: Worked Hypothetical Example  

### Business Profile  

* Small landscaping company with two 5‑ton trucks.  
* Truck A runs 150 mi / week (full schedule).  
* Truck B is idle 3 days / week, used only for occasional overflow jobs (≈ 60 mi / week).  

### Input Data (Truck B)  

| Item | Value |
|------|-------|
| Purchase price | $35,000 |
| Depreciation (5 yr) | $7,000 / yr |
| Fixed insurance (in‑house) | $1,500 / yr |
| Additional rental insurance | $350 / yr |
| Routine maintenance | $1,000 / yr |
| Fuel cost per mile | $0.48 |
| Weekly mileage (current) | 60 mi |
| Market rental rate | $90 / day |
| Market lease rate | $1,400 / mo |
| Admin time per rental | 0.5 hr |
| Hourly cost of admin | $25 / hr |
| Maintenance share for rental | $50 / wk |

### 4.1 In‑House Break‑Even Mileage  

```
Weekly Cost = (7,000 + 1,500 + 1,000) / 52 + (0.48 × Weekly Miles)
Weekly Cost = $173.08 + (0.48 × Weekly Miles)
```
To break even, weekly revenue must exceed $173.08 + 0.48×Miles.  
If the company earns $2.00 per mile on landscaping jobs:  

```
Revenue = 2.00 × 60 = $120
Net = $120 – $173.08 – (0.48×60) = $120 – $173.08 – $28.80 = –$81.88
```
Truck B loses money when used only for 60 mi / week.

### 4.2 Rental Break‑Even Days  

```
Insurance_additional = $350 / 52 ≈ $6.73 per week
Maintenance_share = $50 per week
Admin_cost = 0.5 hr × $25 = $12.50 per rental
Total weekly overhead = $6.73 + $50 + $12.50 = $69.23
Break‑Even Days = $69.23 / $90 ≈ 0.77 days
```
**Result:** Renting the truck for just **one day per week** covers the extra costs and starts generating profit. If demand allows 2‑3 rental days, net profit rises quickly.

### 4.3 Lease Break‑Even Mileage  

```
Lease weekly cash = $1,400 / 4 = $350
Insurance_additional = $6.73 per week
Maintenance_share (lease) = $30 per week (assumed lower)
Net weekly cash after costs = $350 – $6.73 – $30 = $313.27
```
Even if the lease caps mileage at 200 mi / week, the company still pockets $313 weekly, far above the $173 in‑house cost. The lease is profitable regardless of mileage, but it removes the truck from any future own‑use.

### 4.4 Decision Matrix Scores (example)  

| Criterion | Weight | In‑House | Rental | Lease |
|-----------|--------|----------|--------|-------|
| Revenue Potential | 5 | 2 | 4 | 3 |
| Cash‑Flow Predictability | 4 | 2 | 3 | 5 |
| Admin Burden | 3 | 5 | 2 | 3 |
| Insurance Risk | 4 | 3 | 2 | 3 |
| Maintenance Control | 2 | 5 | 3 | 4 |
| Flexibility | 5 | 5 | 2 | 3 |
| **Weighted Total** | — | **?** | **?** | **?** |

Calculating (Score × Weight) gives:

* In‑House: (2×5)+(2×4)+(5×3)+(3×4)+(5×2)+(5×5) = 10+8+15+12+10+25 = **80**  
* Rental: (4×5)+(3×4)+(2×3)+(2×4)+(3×2)+(2×5) = 20+12+6+8+6+10 = **62**  
* Lease: (3×5)+(5×4)+(3×3)+(3×4)+(4×2)+(3×5) = 15+20+9+12+8+15 = **79**

**Interpretation:** The in‑house option scores highest, but only because flexibility and maintenance control are heavily weighted. If cash‑flow predictability is the top priority, increase its weight; the lease would then win.

## Step 5: Choose and Implement  

1. **Set your priority weighting** – decide whether cash flow, flexibility, or admin effort matters most.  
2. **Plug your real numbers** into the break‑even formulas (use a simple spreadsheet).  
3. **Score the matrix** with your weighted criteria.  
4. **Select the option** that yields the highest weighted total *and* meets the break‑even threshold.  

For the landscaping example, if the owner values cash‑flow stability above flexibility, the lease is the logical choice. If occasional own‑use is still needed, renting a few days a week provides profit while preserving some flexibility.

## Trade‑offs, Risks, and Cost Considerations  

| Option | Key Trade‑off | Typical Risk | Mitigation |
|--------|---------------|--------------|------------|
| **In‑House** | Full control vs. opportunity cost | Vehicle sits idle, depreciation continues | Schedule overflow jobs, share vehicle with nearby businesses on an ad‑hoc basis |
| **Rental** | Higher per‑day revenue but adds admin & liability | Damage, late returns, insurance gaps | Use a written rental agreement, require a security deposit, verify renter’s insurance |
| **Lease** | Predictable cash flow, no admin | Loss of flexibility, possible mileage caps, early‑termination penalties | Negotiate mileage allowances, include a buy‑back clause, compare multiple lessors |

Maintenance costs can spike unexpectedly; keep a reserve fund equal to at least one month of operating expenses. Insurance requirements differ by jurisdiction—verify that any third‑party renter or lessee carries the necessary coverage before signing contracts.
